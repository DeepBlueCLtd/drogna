The Background Tab, step by step

Nine explainers of the standards the architecture rests on, drawn out as wireframes: what each step shows, what it says, and what the viewer can poke. This is the authoring brief the spec (FR-016–FR-024) governs, made visible enough to argue with.

Audience evaluator · domain expert · future maintainer Course 9 explainers · 56 steps Budget 60–90s each — about 10s a step Path Next performs the interaction

How to read these

Each row is one step on the explainer’s spine. Pressing Next moves down the rows and drives the mechanism itself, so a viewer who never touches a diagram still sees everything and arrives at the value panel. Poking is a second route to the same states, never the only one.

points — observations, casts, readings fields — gridded, computed everywhere the archive — the coarse prior you already hold
Interactive regions carry a dashed outline — learned once, applied across all nine. Static, so nothing animates on arrival, and it survives greyscale. The rail also shows each explainer’s length, so a viewer knows what they are starting.

Those two hues carry the course’s central distinction, so they mean the same thing in every explainer. In the built version each also carries a texture and a line weight, so the distinction survives greyscale by construction rather than by promise (FR-011).

The grey was wrong in the first draft. It meant “ground truth you never have”, which is romantic and false here: drogna holds a multi-decade monthly historic archive (SRD FR-21), and the generator records ground truth in a manifest so recovery can be scored (Constitution IX). Grey now means the archive — coarse, dated, real, and queryable. That is a better lesson anyway: a data system sharpens a prior, it does not conjure knowledge from nothing.

1

Why a standard at all

slides · 5 steps

A bespoke interface is cheap to build and expensive to live with. The interface should outlive the system behind it.

One of only two explainers that opens on the painful alternative, because here the alternative is genuinely what most people do.

/why-a-standard/1

The interface outlives the system

Every system here will be replaced. What survives a replacement is not the database or the language — it is the agreement about how everyone else reaches it. That agreement is the thing worth choosing carefully.

/why-a-standard/2

The bespoke interface, on the day it ships

It fits your data exactly, because you designed it around your data. It is also the cheapest thing on this page to build. Every consumer needs an adapter, but there are only three of them and you know all three by name.

/why-a-standard/3

Five years later

Nothing dramatic happened. The author moved on. The documentation and the code drifted apart, and only one of them is executable. A fourth consumer arrives and their question is not “how do I call it” but “who can tell me what it does now?” That question has a cost, and it recurs.

/why-a-standard/4

The same picture, with a standard

The consumers did not change. What changed is that they arrived already knowing how to ask. The adapters are not better — they are absent, along with the people who would have maintained them.

this is the criterion the other seven are judged by
/why-a-standard/5

What it delivers

Through-life cost

A bespoke interface is supported by whoever wrote it, for as long as they are there. A standard is supported by everyone who implements it. Argued, not measured.

Interoperability

A consumer that already speaks the standard is not an integration project. That is the entire difference.

What you don’t build

Little to say here. This explainer sets the criterion; the seven that follow are where it is spent.

Note. The omitted third axis is deliberate and is the first thing a reader meets — it teaches that an empty box carries a reason rather than padding (FR-008).
2

Points and fields

interactive · 6 steps

A cast and a forecast are different animals. This is why explainers 4 and 5 are a pair and not a duplication.

Section view throughout — depth down the page, the natural frame for a cast. The load-bearing explainer: if this doesn’t land, SensorThings and EDR read as two ways to do one thing.

/points-and-fields/1

The sea has a temperature everywhere

Warm core here, a front there, the thermocline lying across the whole section. Every point in this water has a real value right now. You have none of them.

section view · depth down the page
/points-and-fields/2

You never have the sea. You have where you looked.

Three casts, eleven readings, each one true and each attached to an instrument, a moment and a depth. Behind them, in grey, is the multi-decade monthly archive — six cells across, years out of date, and the thing you actually had before anyone went to sea. The casts do not fill the gaps. They correct a belief.

poke: drag a cast to a new position and watch what it catches
/points-and-fields/3

A forecast has a value everywhere

Not because anyone went there, but because a model computed it. This is a genuinely different object: complete, regular, and only as good as the model and the observations that fed it.

/points-and-fields/4

Ask them both the same question

“What is the temperature at 200 metres, here?” The field answers immediately, everywhere, always. The observations answer “nothing was measured there” — which is not a failure, and is not the same as a null, and is not the same as a refusal. Three different facts, and an interface that cannot tell them apart is lying to somebody.

the beat — everything downstream depends on this landing poke: move the crosshair and watch both answers change
/points-and-fields/5

So they need different questions asked of them

Points want provenance: which instrument, calibrated when, in what units, measuring what else at the same moment. Fields want geometry: this slice, along this track, at these depths, valid then. Two shapes, two query languages — and now SensorThings and EDR are obviously a pair rather than a redundancy.

/points-and-fields/6

What it delivers

Through-life cost

Choosing two stores and two query languages once is cheaper than discovering it later, at the point where one interface has to answer both badly.

Interoperability

Not this explainer’s. This is about the shape of the data; interoperability arrives with the standards that serve each shape, in 4 and 5.

What you don’t build

Knowing which shape you are holding tells you which standard already exists for it. The recognition is most of the saving.

3

NetCDF

interactive · 6 steps

A file that explains itself. Units, reference frame and time origin travel inside it, so a reader in thirty years needs no surviving author.

/netcdf/1

A block of numbers means nothing

Sixteen values. Are they Celsius or Kelvin? Which way is down? Is the first row the surface or the seabed? Somebody knew, once. Everything that follows is about putting that knowledge inside the file rather than beside it.

/netcdf/2

Dimensions give each number a position

Four axes — longitude, latitude, depth, time. The block is no longer a pile; it is an addressed volume. You still cannot say where on Earth it is.

/netcdf/3

Coordinates put the position on Earth

The axes carry real values now — 14°W to 8°W, the surface down to a thousand metres. A reader can point at one number and say exactly which cubic metre of ocean it claims to describe.

poke: click any cell to see the coordinate it resolves to
/netcdf/4

Attributes make the file answer for itself

Celsius, not Kelvin. WGS 84, not something local. Seconds since 1970, so the timestamps cannot be misread. And a fill value, so “missing” is stated rather than guessed at from a suspicious zero. None of this needs a wiki page, because none of it is outside the file.

/netcdf/5

Thirty years on

Same file, no author, no wiki, no schema registry, no one left who was there. It still opens, and it still says what it is. The alternative is not a worse file format — it is a file plus a person who has moved on.

/netcdf/6

What it delivers

Through-life cost

The strongest case in the course. Self-description removes the standing obligation to keep a separate document true — the obligation that is never funded and always assumed.

Interoperability

Any reader in the ecosystem opens it. No bespoke parser per producer, and no negotiation about what column three meant.

What you don’t build

A metadata sidecar, a schema registry, and the convention document that would have to be written, circulated and then kept in step.

4

What a holding is

interactive · 6 steps

A field is not one thing. A collection spans a coarse archive, a current now-cast, and every forecast run the loop has published — each queryable the same way.

New in this draft. It goes here because knowing what is there precedes knowing how to ask for it — and because the previous three explainers have been quietly saying “the field” as though there were only one.

/holdings/1

A collection is not a file

Ask for sea water temperature and the honest answer is “which one?” — the long coarse record, the current best picture, or one of the forecasts. All three sit under one name, and all three answer the same queries.

/holdings/2

The archive: what you knew before anyone sailed

Two decades of monthly means. Too coarse to navigate by and too old to trust for today — but complete, everywhere, always. This is the grey behind the casts in explainer 2, and it is why a gap in the observations is not a hole in your knowledge.

/holdings/3

The now-cast: one, and current

A single field, replaced on a cadence. There is never a second one to choose between, which makes it the safe default for “what is it like out there?” Its manifest still records what it was derived from — being current is not an excuse for being unexplained.

/holdings/4

Instances: every run you ever published

The loop does not overwrite; it adds. So you can ask not only what the forecast says now, but what it said last Tuesday, and what it was made from — because each run carries the manifest that reconstructs it byte for byte. When someone asks why a decision was taken on Tuesday, that is the only answer that holds up.

the beat — provenance you can replay, not provenance you assert poke: pick a run and see the manifest it carries
/holdings/5

And it says truthfully what it holds

Every collection publishes its extent — the years, the box, the depths. A document that claims more than the store contains is not a documentation bug: it is a client planning a route through water nobody modelled. Here the claim is verified against the store by test, which is a promise most catalogues cannot make.

/holdings/6

What it delivers

Through-life cost

Accumulating runs with manifests means questions about the past are answered by replay rather than by whoever still remembers the configuration.

Interoperability

Three eras behind one collection name and one query language: a client that can read the now-cast can read twenty years of archive without learning anything new.

What you don’t build

The extent-and-discovery vocabulary. Standards already say how a collection states what it holds, so the only work left is telling the truth in it.

5

SensorThings

interactive · 7 steps

Provenance is structural. Every number walks back to the instrument that made it, because that path is the shape of the data rather than a column someone remembered to add.

Vocabulary hazard: the gate forbids two entity nouns this explainer would reach for naturally. A sensor samples; an instrument reports.

/sensorthings/1

A reading, on its own

8.4 at 09:14. You cannot use this. Not because it is wrong, but because you cannot tell whether to trust it, compare it, or correct it. Every question you would ask about it is a question about something else.

/sensorthings/2

The whole model fits on one screen

Eight types, and that is the entire standard. Worth seeing once, because the usual fear about adopting a standard is that it is unbounded — this one you can hold in your head. We walk the six that answer “can I trust this reading”. FeatureOfInterest is the subtle one: where the observation applies, which is not always where the instrument is when water moves past a moored sensor.

/sensorthings/3

It belongs to a stream

Not a table, a stream: one quantity, measured by one instrument, on one platform, over time. The reading is a member of it. That single relationship is what the rest of the chain hangs from.

/sensorthings/4

What it measures, and what did the measuring

The property is the quantity in the world: sea water temperature, defined the same way for everyone. The sensor is the device: its make, its calibration date, how it samples. Two questions that get conflated constantly, kept apart here by construction.

/sensorthings/5

What it is attached to

A Thing is the buoy. It has a location, and it carries several streams at once — temperature, salinity, pressure, all from the same hull at the same moment. That is how you find out what else was happening when a reading looked odd.

/sensorthings/6

Walk it backwards from any number

From one reading to the stream, the instrument, its calibration, the hull it sits on and where that hull was. Not because someone designed a provenance feature, but because the navigation is the data model. A client that has never seen your system can walk it on the first try.

URLs generic to the standard, against a fictional host (FR-022)
/sensorthings/7

What it delivers

Through-life cost

Provenance that is structural cannot rot the way a convention can. There is no separate discipline to sustain, so none to quietly lapse.

Interoperability

A client written against SensorThings works against any SensorThings server — including the ones you have not met yet.

What you don’t build

The entity model, the navigation links, the filter syntax, and the argument about what to call things.

6

OGC API-EDR

interactive · 7 steps · the long one

The query is a geometry. “Conditions along my planned track” is one request, not a client-side loop.

Budget risk. Seven steps against a 60–90 second promise. This is the one explainer that does not obviously fit, and step 3/4 could merge — at the cost of the viewpoint switch being less clearly signposted. Flagged rather than silently crammed; see the open questions at the end.
/edr/1

You want conditions along a planned track

A point interface can only answer about points. So each client picks forty positions, issues forty requests, stitches the answers and interpolates the gaps — and every client does that differently. The disagreements surface later, in an operations room.

the second of only two “old way” openings
/edr/2

Send the shape of the question

EDR’s move is to make the query a geometry. The track goes to the server; the server does the subsetting where the field already is. One request, one answer, and one implementation of the hard part instead of one per client.

/edr/3

The queries that are about depth

A position asks one place at one depth. A vertical profile asks one place all the way down — the shape of a cast, served from a field. Both read naturally in section, which is why they come first.

section view · depth down the page
/edr/4

The queries that are about area

Everything within so many kilometres. Everything inside this polygon. Everything within a stated width of this route. These cannot be drawn in section, so the viewpoint changes and the step says so rather than letting the reader work it out.

plan view · looking down poke: drag the corridor width and watch the catchment change
/edr/5

Trajectory: the one with time in it

Every vertex carries its own time, so the answer is what the water will be like when you get there — sampled from the forecast valid at each moment, not from a single snapshot. This is the query that makes a forecast operationally useful rather than merely correct.

/edr/6

What comes back is the same shape every time

Whatever the geometry of the question, the answer is CoverageJSON: the same structure, the same way of saying where and when each value applies, and the same way of saying “no value here”. A client writes the parsing once.

/edr/7

What it delivers

Through-life cost

One implementation of subsetting and interpolation, beside the data, instead of one per client — each drifting, and each disagreeing in an operations room later.

Interoperability

The query types mean the same thing on every EDR service, so a client aimed at one is aimed at all of them.

What you don’t build

Subsetting, path interpolation, the response format, and the vocabulary for saying a value is absent rather than zero.

7

pygeoapi

slides · 5 steps

One server, many standards, plugin-shaped. New capability arrives as configuration rather than as a project.

Written in the present tense about the real deployment. Step 4 carries FR-013’s obligation: this page is not a pygeoapi instance and must say so.

/pygeoapi/1

One server, several standards

The data stays where it is. In front of it sits a single server that speaks several OGC standards at once, each over the same holdings. You configure collections; you do not write endpoints.

/pygeoapi/2

What arrives without being asked for

Discovery, conformance, queryables, paging, both HTML and JSON of everything. This is the tier of work that is invisible in a demo, appears in no estimate, and takes months when you write it yourself.

/pygeoapi/3

And what it opens next

Tiles when someone wants a fast map client. Records when the holdings need a catalogue. Processes when a derived product should be computed on demand rather than stored. Each is a configuration change, not a procurement.

/pygeoapi/4

Where it does not reach, you extend it

Adoption is not free. Where a standard is newer than its implementations, you write a provider against the plugin seam — drogna wrote two. The cost is bounded and lands in one place, which is a different thing from a bespoke server.

FR-013: also states plainly that this page serves its interfaces in the browser, not through pygeoapi
/pygeoapi/5

What it delivers

Through-life cost

The server is maintained by its community rather than by you, and new capability arrives as configuration rather than as a project with a business case.

Interoperability

Several standards from one deployment, over one set of holdings, consistent with each other by construction.

What you don’t build

The strongest case here: discovery, conformance, content negotiation, paging, the HTML views, and the whole collection machinery.

8

MQTT

interactive · 7 steps

A new consumer is a subscription, not a change to the producer.

No sea in this one — the subject is the topic tree, so the viewpoint question does not arise.

/mqtt/1

Everyone wired to everyone

Each producer holds a list of who wants its output. Adding a consumer means editing, testing and redeploying every producer that consumer cares about — so the thing that ought to be a five-minute change becomes a release.

the second “old way” opening — and this one people really do
/mqtt/2

Publish to a topic, not to a person

The producer says what happened and stops caring. It does not know who is listening, or whether anyone is. That ignorance is the feature — it is what makes the next consumer free.

/mqtt/3

The topic tree carries meaning

Not an opaque channel name: a hierarchy where each level says something — kind of instrument, which platform, which quantity. Design the tree well and the next question is already answerable.

/mqtt/4

One subscription, many streams

+ matches one level, # matches the rest. So “everything from hull 044, whatever the instrument” is a single subscription. The one that does not match matters as much as the three that do.

poke: edit the pattern and watch which topics light
/mqtt/5

What else comes with the broker

A retained message means a client that connects late is handed the current value at once rather than sitting blank until the next publish — which is exactly how this page lights up when you open it. And each message carries a delivery guarantee: fire-and-forget for a reading that will be superseded in a second, exactly-once for one that must not be lost. Both are choices you would otherwise have to build, and would build worse.

/mqtt/6

Adding a consumer changes nothing upstream

A new client subscribes and starts receiving. No producer was edited, no release was cut, nothing was redeployed, and nobody had to be asked. Compare the first step: the same request there was a project.

/mqtt/7

What it delivers

Through-life cost

The cost of a new consumer stops scaling with the number of producers. That is the difference between a system that ages well and one that calcifies.

Interoperability

Topic semantics, wildcards and quality-of-service are portable; clients exist for every language you are likely to need.

What you don’t build

Fan-out, subscription management, reconnection and back-off, and the wire protocol underneath all of it.

9

The control loop

interactive · 7 steps

The loop is observable. Every transition is a message you can watch, which is why the seams are where they are.

The only explainer about behaviour rather than an interface, and the closing impression. It is also the one with no standard to teach — it exists to show where the previous seven sit.

/control-loop/1

Four stations, none of them lit

Sense, decide, act, publish. Nothing is running, so nothing is lit — the same rule the System tab obeys. A box here goes bright because a message arrived, never because a flag says it should.

/control-loop/2

Sense: score the difference

Observations arrive and are compared against the forecast that claimed to predict them. The residual is that gap. One large residual is not news — instruments produce spikes — so the monitor watches for a pattern rather than an event.

/control-loop/3

Decide: and often, decline

Divergence crossing a threshold is a request, not a command. The scheduler applies a minimum interval and a cadence floor, and frequently says no. The refusal is published on the same topic as an acceptance, naming the rule and the margin — because a system that only reports its yeses cannot be debugged.

the honest beat: most of what a loop does is decline to act
/control-loop/4

Act: run it, several times

Not one forecast but a handful, from slightly different starting points. Where they agree, confidence is high; where they fan out, it is not. The spread is not a by-product — it is the answer to “how much should I trust this?”

/control-loop/5

Publish: all at once, or not at all

The run is staged, its bytes checked against the digest recorded for it, and only then does the current pointer move. A mismatch is refused with the mismatch named and the pointer untouched. No reader ever observes half a forecast — which is what makes the query layer above it able to promise anything at all.

poke: push the residual up and watch the loop re-plan rather than replay
/control-loop/6

Was it better than doing nothing?

The reference is persistence: assume tomorrow looks like today, and see whether the model beat that. Usually it does. Sometimes it does not, and the display says so — naming the run that failed to earn its compute rather than averaging it away. A system that only reports its wins cannot be trusted about any of them.

Constitution IX — ground truth is scored, never assumed
/control-loop/7

What it delivers

Through-life cost

Every transition is a message on a topic, so the loop can be watched years later by someone who was not there — without a debugger, a rebuild, or the original author.

Interoperability

Nothing to claim. This arrangement is drogna’s own, not a standard. It is here to show where the seven standards sit, not to be adopted.

What you don’t build

A defence of automated action. The planner recommends and a person disposes, so no one has to justify a decision the machine took alone.

Settled in this pass

The grey was wrong, and the correction improved the course. It meant “ground truth you never have” — romantic, and false here. drogna holds a multi-decade monthly archive (SRD FR-21) and records ground truth in a manifest so recovery can be scored (Constitution IX). Grey now means the coarse prior you already hold. Points and fields step 2 keeps its line and changes its picture: casts sit over an explicit six-cell archive grid, so the drawing no longer implies the sea is unknowable. That correction is what produced explainer 4.
EDR was never over budget. The first draft flagged seven steps as too many. At about ten seconds a step — enough to read two sentences and watch the diagram change — seven steps is roughly seventy seconds, inside the promise. Four explainers now run to seven, so the rail showing length is a general courtesy rather than an exception.
No explainer skips the value panel. FR-008 already handles thinness at the right grain — an omitted axis with a stated reason, which three explainers use. Dropping the whole panel would take the argument out of precisely the four longest and densest explainers, to save a step in ones that were never the problem.

Still open

1 · The course is now 56 steps — about ten minutes end to end. That is a session, not a glance, and this audience was chosen partly for being time-poor. Dip-in is supported, but nobody has decided which three explainers a viewer with four minutes should be steered to.
2 · Two explainers are not about standards. Holdings (4) and the control loop (9) are drogna’s own arrangement. Both earn their place by making the others honest — but the stated frame is “the standards themselves, drogna as worked example”, and two of nine now sit outside it.
3 · FeatureOfInterest is named but not walked. Step 2 calls it the subtle one — where an observation applies is not always where the instrument is — and the walk then skips it. Either it deserves a step or it should not be singled out.
4 · Nothing shows a refusal at the boundary. Default deny is a constitutional principle with a visible surface, and no explainer touches it. It may belong in pygeoapi, or nowhere.
