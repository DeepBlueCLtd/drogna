The Background Tab, step by step

Ten explainers of the standards the architecture rests on — and of what it takes to use them honestly — drawn out as wireframes: what each step shows, what it says, and what the viewer can poke. This is the authoring brief the spec (FR-016–FR-024) governs, made visible enough to argue with.

Audience evaluator · domain expert · future maintainer Course 11 explainers · 69 steps Budget 60–90s each — about 10s a step Path Next performs the interaction

How to read these

Each row is one step on the explainer’s spine. Pressing Next moves down the rows and drives the mechanism itself, so a viewer who never touches a diagram still sees every state and reaches the consequences panel. Interaction is a second route to those states, never the only one.

points — observations, casts, readings fields — gridded, computed everywhere the archive — the coarse prior you already hold
Interactive regions carry a dashed outline — learned once, applied across all nine. Static, so nothing animates on arrival, and it survives greyscale. The rail also shows each explainer’s length, so a viewer knows what they are starting.

Those two hues carry the course’s central distinction, so they mean the same thing in every explainer. In the built version each also carries a texture and a line weight, so the distinction survives greyscale by construction rather than by promise (FR-011).

Grey means the archive, not unknowable truth. The first draft used it for “ground truth you never have”, which is wrong here: drogna holds a multi-decade monthly archive (SRD FR-21) and records ground truth in a manifest so recovery is scored (Constitution IX). Grey is the coarse prior — dated, real and queryable.

1

Why a standard at all

slides · 5 steps

A bespoke interface is cheap to build and expensive to live with. The interface should outlive the system behind it.

One of only two explainers that opens on the painful alternative, because here the alternative is genuinely what most people do.

/why-a-standard/1

The interface outlives the system

Systems get replaced. What outlasts a replacement is the agreement about how other people reach them, so that agreement is worth choosing carefully.

/why-a-standard/2

The bespoke interface, on the day it ships

It fits your data exactly and it is the cheapest option to build. Each consumer needs an adapter, but there are three of them and you know all three.

/why-a-standard/3

Five years later

The author moved on. Documentation and code drifted, and only one of them is executable. A fourth consumer now needs someone who can say what the interface currently does — a cost that recurs with each new consumer.

/why-a-standard/4

The same picture, with a standard

Same consumers, but they arrive already knowing how to ask. The adapters are gone, and so is the work of maintaining them.

this is the criterion the other seven are judged by
/why-a-standard/5

Consequences

Through-life cost

A bespoke interface is supported by whoever wrote it. A standard is supported by everyone who implements it. Argued, not measured.

Interoperability

A consumer that already speaks the standard needs no integration work.

What you don’t build

Little to say here. This explainer sets the criterion; the seven that follow are where it is spent.

Note. The omitted third axis is deliberate and is the first thing a reader meets — it teaches that an empty box carries a reason rather than padding (FR-008).
2

Points and fields

interactive · 6 steps

A cast and a forecast are different animals. This is why explainers 4 and 5 are a pair and not a duplication.

Section view throughout — depth down the page, the natural frame for a cast. The load-bearing explainer: if this doesn’t land, SensorThings and EDR read as two ways to do one thing.

/points-and-fields/1

The sea has a temperature everywhere

A warm core, a front, the thermocline across the section. Every point in this water has a value. You have measured none of them.

section view · depth down the page
/points-and-fields/2

You never have the sea. You have where you looked.

Three casts, eleven readings, each attached to an instrument, a moment and a depth. Behind them in grey is the monthly archive — six cells across and years out of date, but what you held before anyone went to sea. The casts correct it rather than filling gaps in nothing.

poke: drag a cast to a new position and watch what it catches
/points-and-fields/3

A forecast has a value everywhere

Computed rather than measured. A different object from the casts: complete and regular, and only as good as the model and the observations that fed it.

/points-and-fields/4

Ask them both the same question

“What is the temperature at 200 metres, here?” The field answers everywhere. The observations answer “nothing was measured there”, which is distinct from a null and from a refusal. An interface has to be able to say all three separately.

the beat — everything downstream depends on this landing poke: move the crosshair and watch both answers change
/points-and-fields/5

So they need different questions asked of them

Points want provenance: which instrument, calibrated when, in what units, measuring what else at the same time. Fields want geometry: this slice, along this track, at these depths, valid then. Two shapes, two query languages — which is why SensorThings and EDR are a pair rather than a redundancy.

/points-and-fields/6

Consequences

Through-life cost

Choosing two stores and two query languages up front costs less than retrofitting the second when one interface has to answer both.

Interoperability

Not this explainer’s. This is about the shape of the data; interoperability arrives with the standards that serve each shape, in 4 and 5.

What you don’t build

Knowing which shape you hold tells you which standard already covers it.

3

NetCDF

interactive · 6 steps

A file that explains itself. Units, reference frame and time origin travel inside it, so a reader in thirty years needs no surviving author.

/netcdf/1

A block of numbers means nothing

Sixteen values. Celsius or Kelvin? Which way is down? Is the first row the surface or the seabed? The rest of this explainer is about putting those answers inside the file rather than beside it.

/netcdf/2

Dimensions give each number a position

Four axes: longitude, latitude, depth, time. The block is now an addressed volume, though the addresses do not yet correspond to anywhere on Earth.

/netcdf/3

Coordinates put the position on Earth

The axes now carry values: 14°W to 8°W, surface to a thousand metres. Each number resolves to a specific volume of water.

poke: click any cell to see the coordinate it resolves to
/netcdf/4

Attributes make the file answer for itself

Celsius, WGS 84, seconds since 1970, and an explicit fill value so missing data is declared rather than inferred from a suspicious zero. None of it lives outside the file.

/netcdf/5

Thirty years on

Same file, no author, no wiki, no schema registry. It still opens and still describes itself. The alternative is not a worse format but a format that depends on someone who has moved on.

/netcdf/6

Consequences

Through-life cost

Self-description removes the obligation to keep a separate document accurate. That obligation is rarely funded and usually assumed.

Interoperability

Existing readers open it without a per-producer parser.

What you don’t build

A metadata sidecar, a schema registry, and a convention document to keep in step with both.

4

What a holding is

interactive · 6 steps

A field is not one thing. A collection spans a coarse archive, a current now-cast, and every forecast run the loop has published — each queryable the same way.

New in this draft. It goes here because knowing what is there precedes knowing how to ask for it — and because the previous three explainers have been quietly saying “the field” as though there were only one.

/holdings/1

A collection is not a file

Ask for sea water temperature and there are three possible answers: the long coarse record, the current best picture, or one of the forecasts. All three sit under one collection name and answer the same queries.

/holdings/2

The archive: what you knew before anyone sailed

Two decades of monthly means: too coarse to navigate by, too old for today, and complete everywhere. This is the grey behind the casts in explainer 2, and why a gap in the observations is not a gap in what you hold.

/holdings/3

The now-cast: one, and current

A single field, replaced on a cadence, so there is never a second one to choose between. It carries a manifest recording what it was derived from, like every other holding.

/holdings/4

Instances: every run you ever published

The loop adds rather than overwrites, so you can ask what the forecast said last Tuesday and what it was made from. Each run carries the manifest that reconstructs it byte for byte, which is what makes a past decision auditable.

the beat — provenance you can replay, not provenance you assert poke: pick a run and see the manifest it carries
/holdings/5

And it says truthfully what it holds

Every collection publishes its extent: the years, the box, the depths. An over-claiming document is not a documentation defect but a client planning through water nobody modelled. Here the claim is verified against the store by test.

/holdings/6

Consequences

Through-life cost

Questions about past runs are answered by replay rather than by whoever remembers the configuration.

Interoperability

A client that reads the now-cast reads twenty years of archive with no additional code.

What you don’t build

The extent and discovery vocabulary. The remaining work is stating it accurately.

5

SensorThings

interactive · 7 steps

Provenance is structural. Every number walks back to the instrument that made it, because that path is the shape of the data rather than a column someone remembered to add.

Vocabulary hazard: the gate forbids two entity nouns this explainer would reach for naturally. A sensor samples; an instrument reports.

/sensorthings/1

A reading, on its own

8.4 at 09:14. Not wrong, but unusable: nothing here says whether to trust it, what to compare it against, or how to correct it.

/sensorthings/2

The whole model fits on one screen

Eight types, and that is the entire standard. Worth seeing once, because the usual fear about adopting a standard is that it is unbounded — this one you can hold in your head. We walk the seven that answer “can I trust this reading”; HistoricalLocation is named but not walked.

/sensorthings/3

It belongs to a stream

A stream is one quantity, from one instrument, on one platform, over time. The reading is a member of it, and the rest of the chain hangs off this relationship.

/sensorthings/4

What it measures, and what did the measuring

The property is the quantity: sea water temperature, defined the same way everywhere. The sensor is the device, with its make, calibration date and sampling behaviour. The model keeps them separate.

/sensorthings/5

What it is attached to

A Thing is the buoy. It has a location, and carries several streams at once — temperature, salinity, pressure, from the same hull at the same moment, which is how you find out what else was happening when a reading looked odd. Beside it sits the feature of interest: what the reading is about. For a moored instrument in a current, the water it sampled has already moved on, so where the sensor is and where the observation applies are different facts.

/sensorthings/6

Walk it backwards from any number

From one reading to the stream, the instrument, its calibration, the hull and its position. The navigation is the data model rather than an added provenance feature, so a client that has never seen this system can walk it.

URLs generic to the standard, against a fictional host (FR-022)
/sensorthings/7

Consequences

Through-life cost

Structural provenance has no separate convention to sustain, so none to lapse.

Interoperability

A client written against SensorThings works against any SensorThings server.

What you don’t build

The entity model, the navigation links and the filter syntax.

6

OGC API-EDR

interactive · 7 steps · the long one

The query is a geometry. “Conditions along my planned track” is one request, not a client-side loop.

Budget risk. Seven steps against a 60–90 second promise. This is the one explainer that does not obviously fit, and step 3/4 could merge — at the cost of the viewpoint switch being less clearly signposted. Flagged rather than silently crammed; see the open questions at the end.
/edr/1

You want conditions along a planned track

A point interface answers about points. Each client picks forty positions, issues forty requests, stitches the results and interpolates the gaps — and each client does it slightly differently. The disagreements surface later.

the second of only two “old way” openings
/edr/2

Send the shape of the question

EDR makes the query a geometry. The track goes to the server, which does the subsetting where the field already sits: one request, one answer, and one implementation of the interpolation rather than one per client.

/edr/3

The queries that are about depth

A position asks one place at one depth. A vertical profile asks one place at all depths — the shape of a cast, served from a field. Both read naturally in section.

section view · depth down the page
/edr/4

The queries that are about area

Everything within a radius, inside a polygon, or within a stated width of a route. These cannot be drawn in section, so the viewpoint changes here and the step says so.

plan view · looking down poke: drag the corridor width and watch the catchment change
/edr/5

Trajectory: the one with time in it

Every vertex carries its own time, so each is sampled from the forecast valid at that moment rather than from one snapshot. The answer is the water as it will be on arrival.

/edr/6

What comes back is the same shape every time

Whatever the geometry of the question, the response is CoverageJSON: one structure, one way of saying where and when a value applies, and one way of saying there is no value. A client parses it once.

/edr/7

Consequences

Through-life cost

One implementation of subsetting and interpolation beside the data, rather than one per client, each drifting independently.

Interoperability

The query types mean the same thing on every EDR service.

What you don’t build

Subsetting, path interpolation, the response format, and the vocabulary for absent values.

7

pygeoapi

slides · 5 steps

One server, many standards, plugin-shaped. New capability arrives as configuration rather than as a project.

Written in the present tense about the real deployment. Step 4 carries FR-013’s obligation: this page is not a pygeoapi instance and must say so.

/pygeoapi/1

One server, several standards

The data stays where it is. A single server in front of it speaks several OGC standards over the same holdings. You configure collections rather than write endpoints.

/pygeoapi/2

What arrives without being asked for

Discovery, conformance, queryables, paging, and both HTML and JSON representations. This tier of work is invisible in a demo and rarely appears in an estimate.

/pygeoapi/3

And what it opens next

Tiles for a fast map client, Records for a catalogue, Processes for derived products computed on demand. Each is a configuration change rather than a new component.

/pygeoapi/4

Where it does not reach, you extend it

Adoption is not free. Where a standard is newer than its implementations you write a provider against the plugin seam; drogna wrote two. The cost is bounded and lands in one place.

FR-013: also states plainly that this page serves its interfaces in the browser, not through pygeoapi
/pygeoapi/5

Consequences

Through-life cost

The server is maintained upstream, and new capability arrives as configuration.

Interoperability

Several standards from one deployment over one set of holdings.

What you don’t build

Discovery, conformance, content negotiation, paging, HTML views and the collection machinery.

8

MQTT

interactive · 7 steps

A new consumer is a subscription, not a change to the producer.

No sea in this one — the subject is the topic tree, so the viewpoint question does not arise.

/mqtt/1

Everyone wired to everyone

Each producer holds a list of who wants its output. Adding a consumer means editing, testing and redeploying every producer that consumer needs, so a small change becomes a release.

the second “old way” opening — and this one people really do
/mqtt/2

Publish to a topic, not to a person

The producer publishes and does not know who is listening, or whether anyone is. That is what makes the next consumer cheap to add.

/mqtt/3

The topic tree carries meaning

Each level carries meaning: kind of instrument, which platform, which quantity. A well-designed tree makes the next subscription expressible without a schema change.

/mqtt/4

One subscription, many streams

+ matches one level, # matches the rest, so “everything from hull 044, whatever the instrument” is one subscription. The fourth topic does not match, which is how the rule is learned.

poke: edit the pattern and watch which topics light
/mqtt/5

What else comes with the broker

A retained message hands a late-joining client the current value immediately instead of leaving it blank until the next publish; that is how this page populates on load. Each message also carries a delivery guarantee, from fire-and-forget for a reading superseded within a second to exactly-once for one that must not be lost.

/mqtt/6

Adding a consumer changes nothing upstream

A new client subscribes and starts receiving. No producer edited, no release cut, nothing redeployed. In step 1 the same request required changes to every producer.

/mqtt/7

Consequences

Through-life cost

The cost of a new consumer stops scaling with the number of producers.

Interoperability

Topic semantics, wildcards and delivery guarantees are portable across brokers, with clients in most languages.

What you don’t build

Fan-out, subscription management, reconnection and back-off, and the wire protocol.

9

Reads and writes are separate

interactive · 6 steps

Observations arrive by one path and leave by another. The query components cannot write; the ingestion seam does not serve.

Placed here because both paths have now been shown: SensorThings and EDR serve reads, MQTT carries writes. This explainer only has to name the separation.

/cqrs/1

Two paths, one store

Observations arrive over the broker and are written by the ingestion seam. They leave over HTTP, through the query components. The two paths share the store and nothing else.

/cqrs/2

The write path has one writer

Sensors publish; the ingestion seam validates against the message schema and writes. It is the store’s only writer, so the invariants are enforced in one place rather than in every component that might insert a row.

/cqrs/3

The read path cannot write

The query components hold no write capability at all. That is a property of the topology rather than a rule someone enforces, so a defect in query code cannot corrupt stored data.

/cqrs/4

The two loads never contend

A trajectory query spanning twenty years of archive is expensive. A burst of casts arriving at once is expensive in a different way. Because they run on separate paths, neither one slows the other, and each can be tuned, cached or scaled without regard to the other’s behaviour.

the beat — the two sides have different load shapes and different remedies poke: raise either load and watch the other stay flat
/cqrs/5

Acceptance is decided once, at the seam

A reading is stored or it is refused, counted and kept outside the store with its payload and the reason. There is no third state, so there is no quality column on the observation — and no reader has to remember to filter on one.

/cqrs/6

Consequences

Through-life cost

The read side can be replaced without touching ingestion. Swapping the query layer is a read-path change; the store’s invariants are untouched.

Interoperability

The read side is free to be standards-shaped without that shape constraining how data is written or validated.

Cost

Two paths to operate and monitor rather than one, and a data model that serves reads well is not always the one that accepts writes well. The separation is real work, not free.

10

The control loop

interactive · 7 steps

The loop is observable. Every transition is a message you can watch, which is why the seams are where they are.

The only explainer about behaviour rather than an interface, and the closing impression. It is also the one with no standard to teach — it exists to show where the previous seven sit.

/control-loop/1

Four stations, none of them lit

Sense, decide, act, publish. Nothing is running, so nothing is lit, under the same rule the System tab obeys: a box brightens because a message arrived, not because a flag says it should.

/control-loop/2

Sense: score the difference

Observations are compared against the forecast that predicted them, and the residual is the gap. Instruments produce spikes, so the monitor watches for a sustained pattern rather than a single reading.

/control-loop/3

Decide: and often, decline

Divergence crossing a threshold is a request, not a command. The scheduler applies a minimum interval and a cadence floor and frequently declines. The refusal is published on the same topic as an acceptance, naming the rule and the margin.

most scheduler outcomes are refusals, and they are published too
/control-loop/4

Act: run it, several times

A handful of forecasts from slightly different starting points. Where they agree, confidence is higher; where they diverge, lower. The spread is what the uncertainty field is computed from.

/control-loop/5

Publish: all at once, or not at all

The run is staged, its bytes checked against the recorded digest, and only then does the current pointer move. A mismatch is refused by name with the pointer untouched, so no reader observes a partial field.

poke: push the residual up and watch the loop re-plan rather than replay
/control-loop/6

Was it better than doing nothing?

The reference is persistence: assume tomorrow resembles today, then check whether the model beat that. Usually it does. When it does not, the display names the run rather than averaging it into the others.

Constitution IX — ground truth is scored, never assumed
/control-loop/7

Consequences

Through-life cost

Every transition is a message on a topic, so the loop can be observed later without a debugger or a rebuild.

Interoperability

Nothing to claim. This arrangement is drogna’s own, not a standard. It is here to show where the seven standards sit, not to be adopted.

What you don’t build

A justification for autonomous action: the planner recommends and a person decides.

11

What is allowed to leave

interactive · 7 steps

Data that is fine inside is not automatically fine outside. The boundary denies by default, withholds by absence rather than by filtering, and publishes its refusals.

New, and it closes the course. Not a standard — drogna’s own arrangement — but for an evaluator in this domain it is the question asked first, and the course was silent on it.

/boundary/1

Fine inside is not fine outside

Everything so far has been about serving data well; none of it has asked whether a given caller should see a given holding. A run manifest records exactly where and when sampling happened: useful inside, and not something to publish.

/boundary/2

Denied by default, and identically

Everything is refused unless a rule allows it, so adding a collection never exposes it by accident. The refusals are identical: a released path, an unreleased path and a path that never existed all answer the same way to an uncleared caller, so probing yields no information.

poke: type any path and watch the answer not change
/boundary/3

Withholding is absence, not filtering

A holding that must not leave is absent from the released list, so the boundary has no route to it, and nothing rewrites a response body on the way out. The difference is the failure mode: a broken filter leaks quietly, while a route that does not exist cannot open.

/boundary/4

A refusal is published, not swallowed

Every denial goes onto a topic where it can be watched and counted — for the operator, not the caller, who still learns nothing. A silent refusal is indistinguishable from a boundary nobody has tested.

/boundary/5

The worst variable, not the average

Evidence that the boundary holds is computed per released variable, and the gate acts on the worst, reported with the variable name. The aggregate is still shown, since its distance from the worst is informative, but it is not what decides. An average over four variables will hide one that is leaking; this repository has watched that happen.

the beat — the aggregate passes the case the gate exists to catch
/boundary/6

And it is tested from the inside

A boundary that refuses everything passes every refusal test and serves nobody. The tests exercise at least one allowed request as well, checking the response is byte-identical to what upstream produced. A boundary that has never been entered is untested from the inside.

/boundary/7

Consequences

Through-life cost

Binary clearance is testable in a way per-field redaction is not: redaction must be re-proven whenever a field is added.

Interoperability

The boundary does not fork the API. A cleared caller receives the ordinary standard responses byte for byte.

What you don’t build

A redaction engine, and the recurring review of whether it is still correct.

Settled

Grey means the archive. The first draft used it for ground truth the viewer never has, which is wrong here: drogna holds a multi-decade archive and scores against a recorded ground truth. Correcting it produced explainer 4.
EDR was not over budget. At about ten seconds a step, seven steps is seventy seconds. The first draft’s flag was arithmetic not done. Six explainers now run to seven, so the rail shows length for all of them.
The frame is the standards, and what it takes to use them honestly. Explainers 4, 9 and 11 are drogna’s own arrangement rather than a standard, and the course states that rather than implying otherwise.
Consequences, not benefits. The closing panel is headed Consequences and an axis may record a cost: explainer 9 records two paths to operate, and 7 records the bespoke providers. FR-008 changes from three benefit axes to three consequence axes.
No short path, and no explainer skips the panel. Dip-in works and the rail shows lengths; thinness is handled at the axis, not by dropping the panel.

Still open

1 · Eleven explainers, 69 steps. Roughly eleven minutes at ten seconds a step, less now the prose is shorter. The course has grown by nearly 50% in two passes and each addition was justified individually. Nothing has argued for the whole against a reader’s time.
2 · Three of eleven are not standards. Holdings, read/write separation and the boundary. Each earns its place; the frame now admits them, which also means the frame no longer excludes much.
3 · The boundary closes the course, displacing the control loop, which was designed as the last impression. Releasability is what a bid reviewer asks first, so it is defensible — but it was a side effect of adding explainer 11, not a decision taken on its merits.
4 · Explainer 9 asserts independent scaling without measuring it. The two-curves diagram is drawn, not observed. In a repository whose first lesson is that an unwatched check is worth nothing, an unmeasured performance claim is the same shape. Either it is marked as argued rather than measured, like the cost claims, or the running system is made to produce the curves.
